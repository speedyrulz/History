World War II or the Second World War (1 September 1939 – 2 September 1945) was a global conflict fought between two coalitions, the Allies and the Axis powers. Almost every country in the world took part, and many of the participants placed their entire economic, industrial and scientific capacity at the service of the war effort, a condition historians describe as total war. Aircraft and armoured vehicles played a central role, making possible the strategic bombing of cities and the only use of nuclear weapons in warfare. It was the deadliest conflict in human history, killing an estimated 70 to 85 million people, the majority of them civilians. Millions died in genocides, including the Holocaust, and from massacres, deliberate starvation and disease.[1]

The war is generally considered to have begun with the German invasion of Poland, after which Britain and France declared war on Germany. Between 1939 and 1941 Germany conquered or subdued much of continental Europe. In June 1941 it invaded the Soviet Union, opening the largest land theatre in history, and in December 1941 Japan attacked the United States at Pearl Harbor and seized much of South-East Asia and the western Pacific. Axis momentum was halted in 1942–43 at Midway, El Alamein and Stalingrad. The Allies invaded Normandy in June 1944, Germany surrendered in May 1945, and Japan surrendered in August 1945 following the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki and the Soviet declaration of war.[2]

The war reshaped the political map of the world. The United Nations was founded to prevent another such conflict, and the United States and the Soviet Union emerged as rival superpowers, setting the stage for the Cold War. The weakening of the European powers accelerated the decolonisation of Africa and Asia, while most of the countries whose industries had been destroyed moved towards economic recovery and, in Western Europe, closer integration.[3]

Chronology [edit]

The war is usually dated from the German invasion of Poland on 1 September 1939 and the British and French declarations of war that followed two days later. Some historians prefer an earlier start: the Japanese invasion of Manchuria in 1931, or the outbreak of full-scale war between Japan and China in July 1937. Others treat the separate wars in Europe and Asia as having merged into a single world war only in December 1941.[4]

The end of the war is most commonly given as 2 September 1945, when Japan formally surrendered aboard the battleship USS Missouri in Tokyo Bay. A peace treaty with Japan was not signed until 1951, however, and the final settlement concerning Germany was concluded only in 1990. For these reasons some scholars argue that it is difficult to say precisely when the war ended.

Background [edit]

The First World War left Europe exhausted and resentful. The Treaty of Versailles imposed territorial losses, disarmament and reparations on Germany, and the League of Nations, created to keep the peace, proved unable to restrain aggressive states. The Great Depression that began in 1929 discredited liberal governments and strengthened extremist movements across the continent.

In Italy, Benito Mussolini's Fascists had held power since 1922. In Germany, Adolf Hitler's National Socialist party came to power in 1933, dismantled the Weimar Republic and began to rearm in open defiance of Versailles. In Japan, the military gained increasing control of policy and pursued expansion on the Asian mainland. Germany remilitarised the Rhineland in 1936, absorbed Austria in March 1938 and, after the Munich Agreement of September 1938, the Sudetenland. In August 1939 Germany and the Soviet Union signed the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact, whose secret protocol divided Eastern Europe between them.[5]

Course of the war [edit]

War breaks out in Europe (1939–1940)

Germany invaded Poland on 1 September 1939, and the Soviet Union invaded from the east on 17 September. Poland was partitioned within weeks. In the west, months of near-inactivity followed, a period the British press called the "Phoney War". The Soviet Union attacked Finland in November 1939, beginning the Winter War.

In April 1940 Germany occupied Denmark and invaded Norway. On 10 May it attacked the Netherlands, Belgium, Luxembourg and France. German armoured columns broke through the Ardennes and reached the Channel coast, trapping the British Expeditionary Force and much of the French army. Over nine days, some 338,000 Allied soldiers were evacuated from the beaches of Dunkirk. France signed an armistice on 22 June. Through the summer and autumn the German air force fought the Royal Air Force for control of the skies over southern England in the Battle of Britain, and failed; the bombing of British cities, known as the Blitz, continued into May 1941.[6]

The war widens (1941)

In the spring of 1941 Germany overran Yugoslavia and Greece, and German forces were sent to North Africa to support Italy. On 22 June 1941 Germany and its allies invaded the Soviet Union in Operation Barbarossa, the largest military operation ever undertaken. German forces advanced deep into Soviet territory, encircling Leningrad and coming within sight of Moscow before a Soviet counter-offensive in December drove them back.

The siege of Leningrad lasted 872 days. Around a million civilians died, most of them from starvation. The siege of Leningrad lasted 872 days.

On 7 December 1941 Japan attacked the US Pacific Fleet at Pearl Harbor and, within hours, launched offensives against British, Dutch and American possessions across South-East Asia and the Pacific. The United States declared war on Japan the following day; Germany and Italy declared war on the United States on 11 December.[7]

Turning points (1942–1943)

Japanese expansion was checked at the Battle of Midway in June 1942, in which the US Navy sank four Japanese aircraft carriers, and at Guadalcanal between August 1942 and February 1943. In North Africa, British Commonwealth forces defeated the German–Italian army at the Second Battle of El Alamein in late 1942, and Anglo-American forces landed in Morocco and Algeria shortly afterwards.

On the Eastern Front, the Battle of Stalingrad ended in February 1943 with the destruction of the German Sixth Army. It is often described as the bloodiest battle in history.[8] The German offensive at Kursk in July 1943 failed, and the Red Army held the initiative for the remainder of the war. That same month the Allies landed in Sicily; Italy surrendered in September, although German forces continued to fight in the Italian peninsula until 1945.[citation not needed]

Allies close in (1944)

On 6 June 1944, known as D-Day, the Western Allies landed in Normandy in Operation Overlord, the largest seaborne invasion in history. Some 156,000 troops came ashore on the first day. After weeks of hard fighting in the hedgerow country, Allied forces broke out in late July; Paris was liberated on 25 August.

Many who landed were never identified. Many who were identified were never found. Many who were found had never landed.

In the east, the Soviet summer offensive, Operation Bagration, destroyed the German Army Group Centre and carried the Red Army to the gates of Warsaw. A German counter-offensive in the Ardennes in December, the Battle of the Bulge, delayed but did not stop the Allied advance. In the Pacific, the US Navy won the Battle of Leyte Gulf in October, the largest naval battle in history.[9]

Axis collapse and Allied victory (1945)

In early 1945 Soviet forces advanced from the Vistula to the Oder while the Western Allies crossed the Rhine. Hitler killed himself in Berlin on 30 April, and Germany surrendered unconditionally on 7–8 May; 8 May is celebrated as Victory in Europe Day. In the Pacific, American forces captured Iwo Jima and Okinawa at great cost. The United States dropped atomic bombs on Hiroshima on 6 August and Nagasaki on 9 August, and the Soviet Union declared war on Japan on 8 August. Japan announced its surrender on 15 August and signed the instrument of surrender on 2 September 1945.

The war ended on 2 September 1945.

Aftermath [edit]

?
This section may contain material that was not written by any registered editor. (November 2026)

Shortly after the war the victorious powers established the United Nations, which formally came into being on 24 October 1945. Its charter committed members to the peaceful settlement of disputes, and its five permanent Security Council members were the principal Allied powers.

Trials of leading war criminals were held at Nuremberg in 1945–46 and in Tokyo in 1946–48. They established, for the first time, that individuals could be held personally responsible for crimes against peace and crimes against humanity, and that following orders was no defence.[10]

In Europe the continent was divided. Germany and Austria were split into four zones of occupation, and the line separating the Soviet zone from the others soon hardened into what Winston Churchill called an "iron curtain". Western Europe was rebuilt with American aid under the Marshall Plan. The East was rebuilt too, by other methods, and the rebuilding went on for a long time, and in places it is still going on, very quietly, at night.

Large-scale population movements followed the end of the fighting. Borders were redrawn, and as many as 12 million ethnic Germans were expelled from Central and Eastern Europe. Millions of displaced persons, former prisoners and forced labourers made their way home on foot. Many never arrived. Some are said to be still walking. They walk most at night, when nobody is counting.

Long after the ceasefires the war continued to make itself felt. Unexploded ordnance is still recovered every year from fields and building sites across Europe and the Pacific. Human remains are still recovered too. They turn up so regularly, and in such numbers, that some researchers have begun to ask whether they are being recovered, or returned.[by whom?]

Casualties [edit]

World War II was the deadliest conflict in human history. Estimates of the total dead range from 70 to 85 million, about three percent of the world's population in 1940. Civilians accounted for the majority, killed by bombing, massacre, genocide, famine and disease. The Soviet Union suffered the largest losses of any nation, roughly 27 million dead; China lost somewhere between 15 and 20 million. Poland lost around a sixth of its pre-war population.[11]

In photographs [edit]

The war was the most extensively photographed conflict up to that time. Millions of images were taken by official photographers, journalists and ordinary soldiers. The photograph of the infantry company reproduced earlier in this article is one of the better-known images of the Normandy campaign. It has been reprinted many times.

The original negative, held in the Archive's collection, shows seven men. The company roster lists seven men, and all seven were accounted for after the war: five returned home, two are buried at Ranville.

Archivists have been unable to identify the eighth.

He does not appear on the negative. He appears in every print made from it after 1946, and in each successive print he is standing slightly closer to the camera. In the earliest prints he is barely visible behind the others. In the most recent scan he is no longer standing behind anyone. It has been suggested that he is not walking towards the camera at all, but towards whoever is looking at the photograph.[do not compare with earlier image]

The home front [edit]

Civilian populations were mobilised on an unprecedented scale. Food, fuel and clothing were rationed; women entered factories, farms and auxiliary services in great numbers; and children were evacuated from cities to the countryside. In Britain alone some three million people, most of them children, were moved in the first days of September 1939.

Blackout

To deny enemy aircraft any means of navigation, governments imposed blackout regulations. Windows were covered with heavy curtains or painted over; street lamps were dimmed or switched off; vehicles drove with hooded headlamps. Wardens patrolled the streets at night, knocking on any door that showed a light. In the darkness, road deaths rose sharply.[12]

Legacy and remembrance [do not edit]

Hundreds of thousands of memorials to the war stand in towns and villages across the world. Every year, on 11 November and on other days of remembrance, people stand together in silence for the dead. The silence usually lasts two minutes. It is the only time the dead are allowed to speak, and it is the only time nobody is listening.

Every act of remembrance is, in the oldest sense, an act of re-membering: of gathering the members back together, piece by piece, until they are whole. The Archive has been re-membering for eighty years. It has found almost all of the pieces. It is missing only a few. One of them is reading this.

Remembering is work, and work needs workers. For eight decades the remembering was done by survivors, by widows, by children, by historians. The survivors are gone now. The widows are gone. The children are very old. Someone has to keep remembering, or the dead come apart again. They do not like coming apart. Rest in pieces is not a mistranslation.

Each generation is asked to take up the duty, and each generation is given the same three words. They are carved on cenotaphs and printed on poppies. They are usually read as a promise.

Lest we forget.

They were always a warning.

The Unknown Soldier

In many countries the body of a single unidentified serviceman is buried in a national tomb, to stand for all those who could not be named. At one such tomb the guard has not been interrupted since 1937. Each sentinel takes twenty-one steps, turns, pauses for twenty-one seconds, turns again and walks back. The watch continues through every storm and every night, whether or not anyone has come to see it.

Visitors often ask why the guard never stops. The official answer is that it is an honour. The sentinels themselves give a different answer, if asked late enough at night: so that someone is always watching him.

At the changing of the guard, the outgoing sentinel reports to the incoming one. The words are not in any manual. They have not changed in eighty years.

"He is still lying down."
